Social Security Tax Basics in 2026

Up to 85% of Social Security benefits (retirement, SSDI, and survivors) may be federally taxable based on your combined income. Most states do not tax benefits. This guide covers which benefits are taxed, the exact thresholds, how the taxable amount is calculated, voluntary withholding with Form W-4V, the new senior deduction, and practical examples.

Which Benefits Are Taxed?

  • Retirement, SSDI (Disability), and Survivors benefits — All follow the same federal taxation rules based on combined income.
  • SSI (Supplemental Security Income) — Never taxable at the federal level.

2026 Note: The 2.8% COLA increases the dollar amount of benefits, which raises combined income and can cause more beneficiaries to enter the taxable range even if their other income stays the same.

Are Your Benefits Taxable? (Combined Income Thresholds)

Combined income = Your Adjusted Gross Income (AGI) + nontaxable interest + ½ of your Social Security benefits.

The thresholds have remained unchanged for many years:

2026 Federal Taxation Thresholds for Social Security Benefits
Filing Status Combined Income Taxable Portion of Benefits
Single / Head of Household Under $25,000 0%
Single / Head of Household $25,000 – $34,000 Up to 50%
Single / Head of Household Over $34,000 Up to 85%
Married Filing Jointly Under $32,000 0%
Married Filing Jointly $32,000 – $44,000 Up to 50%
Married Filing Jointly Over $44,000 Up to 85%

Quick Example: Single retiree receives $18,000 in Social Security benefits, has $22,000 AGI and $2,000 of nontaxable interest. Combined income = $22,000 + $2,000 + ($18,000 ÷ 2) = $33,000 → falls in the 50% tier.

How the Taxable Amount Is Calculated

The IRS does not simply take 50% or 85% of your benefits. You must complete the Social Security Benefits Worksheet (found in the Form 1040 instructions). Here is the clear process:

  1. Calculate your combined income
    Combined Income = AGI + nontaxable interest + (½ × Social Security benefits)
  2. Determine which tier you fall into using the thresholds in the table above (0%, up to 50%, or up to 85%).
  3. Apply the worksheet formulas (simplified overview):
    • If you are in the 50% tier, the taxable amount is the lesser of:
      • 50% of your benefits, or
      • 50% of the amount by which combined income exceeds the lower threshold ($25,000 single / $32,000 joint)
    • If you are in the 85% tier, a more detailed two-step calculation applies. The taxable amount is the lesser of:
      • 85% of your benefits, or
      • The sum of: (a) the amount calculated under the 50% rules, plus (b) 85% of the amount by which combined income exceeds the upper threshold ($34,000 single / $44,000 joint)

Important: The taxable portion is added to your other income and then taxed at ordinary income tax rates. It is never taxed at a flat 50% or 85% rate.

2026 Senior Deduction Tip: For tax years 2025–2028, individuals age 65 or older may claim an extra $6,000 deduction ($12,000 if both spouses qualify and file jointly). This deduction phases out beginning at $75,000 modified AGI ($150,000 joint). Because it lowers your overall taxable income, it can reduce or completely eliminate the tax you ultimately pay on Social Security benefits even if a portion of the benefits is still included in income.

Voluntary Tax Withholding (Form W-4V – Rev. January 2026)

You can have federal income tax withheld directly from your Social Security payments to avoid a large tax bill or underpayment penalties later.

  • Available rates: 7%, 10%, 12%, or 22% (you choose one rate only).
  • How to start, change, or stop:
    • Download Form W-4V and submit it to the Social Security Administration, or
    • Use the online option inside your my Social Security account (preferred when available).
  • To stop withholding, submit a new W-4V and check “Do not withhold any federal income tax.”

Example: Monthly benefit $2,200 → choose 10% withholding → $220 is withheld and $1,980 is deposited each month.

Filing Your Taxes & Form SSA-1099

Every January the Social Security Administration issues Form SSA-1099 showing the total benefits you received. You can also download it from your my Social Security account (usually available in early February).

  • Report total benefits received on Form 1040, Line 6a.
  • Report the taxable portion (from the worksheet) on Line 6b.

Tip: Most tax software and IRS Free File will walk you through the Social Security Benefits Worksheet automatically. Always verify your state rules — the majority of states fully exempt Social Security benefits.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do states tax Social Security benefits?

Most do not. A minority of states still tax benefits in full or in part. Check your state tax agency for the latest rules.

Can I reduce taxes on my benefits?

Yes. Options include managing the timing of other income, Roth conversions in lower-income years, and claiming the temporary extra $6,000 senior deduction (age 65+). A tax professional can model your specific situation.

What if I am missing my SSA-1099?

Log into my Social Security and download it. The form is typically available in early February.

Is SSI taxable?

No. Supplemental Security Income is never federally taxable.

What percentage of my benefits can be taxed?

Depending on combined income, 0%, up to 50%, or up to 85% of the benefits themselves may be included in your taxable income. The actual tax paid depends on your ordinary income tax bracket.
